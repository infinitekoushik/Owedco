R&D Tax Credit for Biotech & Pharma Companies: The 2026 Complete Guide

By Owedco Research Team  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  12 min read

If you run a biotech or pharmaceutical company — even a small one, even one that isn't profitable yet — the federal R&D tax credit is probably the single most valuable tax benefit available to you. And most founders and CFOs either don't know how large it can be or are leaving money on the table because they're not documenting it correctly.

This guide covers exactly what qualifies, how the credit is calculated, what biotech-specific pitfalls to avoid and how much your company is likely owed.

Why Biotech & Pharma Qualify So Well

The federal R&D tax credit under IRC Section 41 was designed with exactly your industry in mind. Drug discovery, clinical formulation, biologics development, diagnostic tool creation, device prototyping — these activities sit squarely in the IRS's definition of qualified research.

What makes biotech uniquely positioned is the scale of qualifying expenses. A software company might have $300k in developer salaries that qualify. A biotech company might have $2M in researcher wages, $500k in lab supplies and reagents and $400k in contracted research at a CRO — all potentially qualifying. The credit percentages are the same but the base is far larger.

The Four-Part Test — Does Your Research Qualify?

Every activity must meet all four criteria under IRC Section 41(d) to count as qualified research:

  • Permitted purpose: Developing or improving a product, process, software, formula or technique
  • Technological in nature: Based on principles of biological, chemical, physical or computer science
  • Elimination of uncertainty: You're trying to discover information that eliminates technical uncertainty
  • Process of experimentation: You're testing hypotheses, iterating and evaluating alternatives

Biotech and pharma companies almost universally pass all four tests. The harder question is documentation — which we cover later.

What Expenses Qualify for Biotech & Pharma Companies

1. Employee Wages (Biggest Category)

Any W-2 employee who spends time on qualified research activities contributes their wages to your QRE (qualified research expenses) base. This includes:

  • Research scientists and lab technicians
  • Principal investigators and project leads
  • Biostatisticians and data scientists working on research datasets
  • Regulatory affairs staff working on IND/NDA submissions
  • Quality assurance staff involved in research-phase validation
  • Executives who directly supervise or manage research activities (percentage of time)

You don't need to count their entire salary — only the percentage of time spent on qualifying activities. A PI who spends 80% of their time on qualifying research contributes 80% of their wages.

2. Lab Supplies and Reagents

Supplies consumed in the research process qualify. This is often a significant category for biotech companies:

  • Reagents, chemicals, and biological materials
  • Cell lines, viral vectors and assay kits
  • Lab consumables (plates, tips, tubes, membranes)
  • Prototype materials for device development

Important: Equipment and capital assets do not qualify as supplies. The cost of a centrifuge goes on your balance sheet, not your QRE. But the reagents used in that centrifuge do.

3. Contract Research (65% Rule)

If you outsource research to a CRO, academic lab or independent contractor, 65% of what you pay them counts toward your QREs — provided you own the research results and bear the financial risk if it fails. This is a major benefit for biotech companies that use CROs heavily.

⚠️ CRO Contract Language Matters

Your contract must establish that your company retains rights to the research results. If the CRO owns the IP, you cannot claim the credit. Review your contracts with your tax advisor before claiming CRO expenses.

4. What Does NOT Qualify

How the Credit Is Calculated

There are two methods for calculating the R&D credit. Most biotech companies use one of these:

Regular Credit Method (RC)

This gives you 20% of the increase in QREs above a base amount. The base amount is calculated from your historical R&D spending as a percentage of gross receipts, which makes it complex — but often the larger credit for established companies.

Alternative Simplified Credit (ASC) — Most Common

14% of QREs that exceed 50% of your average QREs over the prior 3 years. If you have no prior-year QREs, the rate is 6% of current-year QREs. This is simpler to calculate and the method most small biotech companies use.

Example Calculation — Early-Stage Biotech

A 3-year-old biotech company has:

  • $1.2M in researcher wages (QRE)
  • $300k in lab supplies (QRE)
  • $400k in CRO payments → $260k qualifies (65%)
  • Total QRE: ~$1.76M
  • Prior 3-year average QRE: $800k
  • 50% of prior average: $400k
  • Excess QRE: $1.76M − $400k = $1.36M
  • ASC Credit: $1.36M × 14% = $190,400

Pre-Revenue Biotech: The Payroll Tax Offset

Most early-stage biotech companies aren't profitable. They have no income tax liability to offset. Does that mean the R&D credit is useless to them?

No. Under the PATH Act (extended and expanded by the Inflation Reduction Act), startups that meet two criteria can apply the R&D credit against their employer-side payroll taxes instead:

  1. Gross receipts under $5 million in the current year
  2. No gross receipts before the 5th year preceding the current tax year

The cap is $500,000 per year (doubled from $250k in 2023). This is real cash — it reduces what you owe on Form 941 every quarter. For a biotech company burning $3M/year with a $1M payroll, this can be a meaningful quarterly benefit.

How to Elect the Payroll Offset

You elect this on your business tax return (Form 6765, Section D). The credit then flows to Form 8974, which you attach to your quarterly payroll tax returns (Form 941). Most payroll processors can handle this once your accountant sets it up.

Documentation: Where Biotech Companies Go Wrong

The IRS doesn't automatically challenge R&D credits, but if you're audited, documentation is everything. Biotech companies have an advantage here: most already keep detailed research records. The challenge is mapping those records to the IRS's framework.

What You Need to Document

  • Project records: Lab notebooks, study protocols, experiment logs, hypothesis documentation
  • Time allocation: Records (formal or informal) of how employees split their time between research and non-research activities
  • Business component: What product, process or technique were you trying to develop or improve?
  • Uncertainty: What technical questions were you trying to answer? What did you not know at the start?
  • Experimentation: What alternatives did you test? What failed? What pivoted?

The "Contemporaneous Records" Problem

The IRS prefers documentation created at the time the research occurred — not reconstructed later. This doesn't mean you need perfect records from day one, but it does mean you should start capturing this information now if you haven't been.

Practically: if your scientists keep lab notebooks and you have project management records (Notion, Jira, lab management software), you're in better shape than you think. A qualified R&D tax specialist can help you organize existing records into a defensible study.

Should You Do This Yourself or Use a Specialist?

For biotech and pharma companies, this is genuinely a specialist's job. The credit calculation is complex, the exclusions (funded research, foreign research, post-approval activities) require careful analysis, and a poorly prepared credit is worse than no credit — it creates audit risk without the benefit of a clean study.

The good news: most R&D tax credit specialists work on contingency. You pay nothing upfront. They calculate your credit, prepare the study, and take a percentage of what they recover. For a company with $1M+ in annual QREs, the fee is well worth it.

What to look for in a firm:

  • Experience specifically in biotech and life sciences (the nuances around clinical trials, CRO treatment and funded research require industry knowledge)
  • Willingness to provide a free preliminary estimate
  • Clear explanation of their documentation methodology
  • Audit support included in their fee structure

Biotech R&D Credit: Frequently Asked Questions

Does a Phase III trial qualify for the credit?

Partially. Activities in Phase III that still involve eliminating technical uncertainty — protocol development, data analysis, formulation work — typically qualify. Routine data collection and post-approval studies generally don't. This needs to be analyzed activity-by-activity.

We received an NIH grant. Does that eliminate our credit?

Only for the research funded by the grant. The funded research exclusion means you can't claim the credit for activities where the government (or another party) funded the work and bears the financial risk. But if you have any self-funded research activities, those still qualify.

We're incorporated in Delaware but our lab is in another state. What states offer additional credits?

Most states with significant biotech clusters offer their own R&D credits: California (15% for small businesses), Massachusetts (10%), Maryland (10%), New Jersey (10%), Texas (none, but no state income tax) and others. These are stacked on top of the federal credit.

Our lead molecule failed in Phase II. Did we waste the documentation effort?

No. The credit applies to the research activity, not the outcome. Failed experiments are still qualified research. The IRS explicitly does not require that your research succeed — only that you were following a process of experimentation aimed at resolving technical uncertainty.
