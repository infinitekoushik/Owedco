R&D Tax Credit for Startups — How to Use It Against Your Payroll Taxes

Most startups aren't profitable yet — which makes tax credits seem useless. There's a version of the R&D credit designed specifically for that situation and almost no one uses it.

The standard R&D tax credit reduces your income tax bill. That's great if you're profitable. If you're a pre-revenue or early-stage startup burning through runway, a credit against income tax you don't owe yet isn't much use.

Congress recognised this in 2015 and created a specific provision for startups: the ability to apply R&D credits against payroll taxes instead. This means real cash coming back — not a future tax benefit — even if you've never turned a profit and have no federal income tax liability at all.

Who qualifies for the payroll tax version

To use the R&D credit against payroll taxes, your business needs to meet two conditions:

  • You have been in existence for fewer than five years — meaning fewer than five years since you first had gross receipts
  • Your annual gross receipts are below $5 million

That's it. If you're building a product, have a small engineering team and are under five years old with under $5M in revenue, you very likely qualify — both for the R&D credit itself and for the payroll tax election.

The IRS doubled the maximum payroll tax offset from $250,000 to $500,000 per year starting in 2023. That change is still in effect for the 2026 tax year. Pre-2023 articles you find online will have the old, lower figure.

How the numbers actually work

The R&D credit is calculated at roughly 6–8% of your qualified research expenses — wages for your engineers and developers, contractor costs and supplies used in development. Then you elect to apply that credit against your employer portion of Social Security taxes (the 6.2% you pay on top of each employee's wages).

That $42,000 isn't a deduction from taxable income — it's a direct offset against taxes you're writing a cheque for every payroll cycle. For a startup paying $800,000 in engineering payroll, that's a 5.25% reduction in total compensation cost.

Which startup activities qualify

The same four-part IRS test applies here as it does for larger companies. For a typical software or tech startup, the following activities qualify:

  • Building and iterating on your core product — especially early-stage work where the technical approach wasn't obvious
  • Developing infrastructure, APIs or backend systems that required genuine engineering problem-solving
  • Prototyping features that were later abandoned — failed attempts still count
  • Technical work on performance, scalability or reliability challenges
  • Any work where your engineers were figuring out how to do something, not just executing a known method

Time spent on fundraising, sales, marketing or general management doesn't qualify. But in a typical early-stage startup where most of the headcount is technical, a large portion of your payroll will likely count.

How to actually claim it — the process

You calculate your R&D credit on Form 6765 when you file your annual tax return. On that form, there's an election to apply the credit against payroll taxes instead of income taxes. The elected amount then flows to Form 941 — your quarterly payroll tax return — and offsets what you owe for employer Social Security taxes.

The practical steps look like this:

  1. Work with a tax credit specialist to identify your qualifying activities and calculate your qualified research expenses for the year
  2. File Form 6765 with your annual return, making the payroll tax election
  3. Starting with the first quarter after you file, your Form 941 payments are reduced by the elected credit amount until it's fully used

The timing matters: the election has to be made on a timely-filed return. You can't go back and add it after the fact for the current year, though you can amend prior returns to claim credits you missed.

The most common mistake startups make

Waiting. Startups often plan to "deal with the R&D credit when we're profitable." That costs them real money in two ways: first, they miss out on the payroll tax benefit that's available right now; second, they lose the ability to amend prior years that fall outside the three-year lookback window.

A startup that's been operating for two years without claiming the credit has two years of prior claims available right now, on top of the current year. Three years in, that first year starts to expire.
