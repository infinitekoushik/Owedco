R&D Tax Credit Audits and Non-Qualifying Activities: What You Need to Know (2026)

The IRS pays more attention to R&D credit claims than to almost any other business tax item. That does not mean you should not claim it. It means you should claim it correctly. Here is exactly what gets people audited, which activities get thrown out, and what documentation actually protects you.

Why R&D Credit Claims Get Audited More Than Other Deductions

The R&D tax credit is one of the largest business tax credits in the US tax code. The IRS knows this. They also know that plenty of businesses overclaim, misclassify activities, or file Form 6765 without the documentation to back it up.

In 2021 the IRS issued a significant update to their information document request process for R&D credit refund claims. Filers now have to provide detailed descriptions of the research activities, identify the business components involved, name the individuals who did the research, and specify the information the business was trying to discover. This is not optional. It is a requirement before any refund claim gets processed.

The IRS Large Business and International division has an entire team that reviews R&D credit claims. For smaller businesses, correspondence audits triggered by the credit are not uncommon. This is not a reason to skip the credit. It is a reason to document your claim like someone is going to ask about every line item. Because they might.

The Non-Qualifying Activities That Get Claims Thrown Out

Most audit adjustments come down to the same handful of mistakes. Companies include activities that sound like research but fail one or more parts of the IRS four-part test. Here is what gets disallowed most often.

Activities the IRS routinely disallows

  • Quality control testing of existing products or processes with no intent to improve them
  • Routine data collection, even if sophisticated software is involved
  • Research conducted after commercial production begins (post-commercial development)
  • Adapting existing products for a specific customer's requirements
  • Efficiency studies or productivity analysis of existing operations
  • Management or organizational studies, business process improvement without technical uncertainty
  • Market research, consumer surveys, and focus group testing
  • Style, taste, cosmetic, or aesthetic changes with no functional technical challenge
  • Research funded by another party under a contract (the funder gets the credit, not the performer)
  • Social science research, economics research, humanities research
  • Research outside the United States or its territories
  • Research related to internal-use software that fails the high-threshold-of-innovation test
  • Reverse engineering a competitor's product
  • Reproduction of an existing business component with no technical uncertainty

The Four-Part Test: Where Most Claims Break Down

Every activity you include in an R&D credit claim has to pass all four parts of the IRS test. One failure disqualifies the whole activity. Here is where businesses most often stumble.

Part 1: Permitted Purpose

The research must relate to a new or improved business component: a product, process, software, technique, formula, or invention. Research aimed at cost reduction for its own sake, compliance research, or market-positioning research does not qualify. The research has to be aimed at creating or improving a functional product or process.

Part 2: Technological in Nature

The research must rely on principles of physical, biological, computer, or engineering science. Business process improvement that does not involve technical principles fails here. Developing a new sales methodology is not technological in nature. Developing the software that runs that methodology might be.

Part 3: Elimination of Uncertainty

This is the most commonly failed test in audits. You have to be trying to discover information that eliminates technical uncertainty about whether something can be developed, or about the appropriate design of something. If your team already knew how to build it and just executed on that knowledge, there was no uncertainty to eliminate. The IRS will ask for evidence that you faced genuine uncertainty at the start of the project.

Part 4: Process of Experimentation

There has to be a systematic process of evaluating alternatives. Trial and error qualifies. Modeling and simulation qualify. A single approach executed without alternatives does not. Companies that went straight from concept to production without testing alternatives often lose this part of the test in audits.

The most dangerous mistake: Including activities that passed parts 1, 2, and 3 but where the team had a single predetermined solution and just built it. No process of experimentation means no credit, even if everything else looks right.

What Actually Triggers an IRS Audit on R&D Credits

Large first-time claims with no prior credit history

Filing a $200,000 R&D credit for the first time when you have never claimed one before is a flag. It signals the possibility that someone found the credit and went back to maximize it aggressively. Not wrong to do this, but be ready to document every dollar.

Credit as a percentage of revenue that looks unusually high

If your R&D credit claim represents a very large share of your gross revenue, the IRS notices. There is no bright-line rule here but ratios that look out of place for your industry draw scrutiny.

Amended returns claiming R&D credits for multiple prior years simultaneously

Going back and amending three years of returns to add R&D credits is completely legal. It also flags all three years for review at the same time. Have your documentation ready for all three before you file the amendments.

100% of an employee's time claimed as R&D

Claiming that your CEO, CFO, or a sales manager spent 100% of their time on qualifying research is a red flag. The IRS knows what those roles actually involve. Be conservative and accurate with time allocation.

Supplies claimed at unusually high percentages of total expenses

If your supply QREs are 80% of your total claim and wages are 20%, that is an unusual ratio. Most legitimate claims are dominated by wages. High supply percentages invite questions about what those supplies were and how they were consumed in research.

No contemporaneous documentation

Filing Form 6765 and then recreating your records after the fact if you get audited is a losing strategy. The IRS can tell the difference between records that were kept as work happened and records assembled after the fact. Contemporaneous documentation is not optional.

The Activities That Look Like R&D But Are Not

Activity Status Why
Debugging software to fix known errors in production code Disqualified Fixing known defects is maintenance, not research. No uncertainty being eliminated.
Building features customers specifically requested under a contract Risky Funded research rules may apply. If the customer owns the results, the credit belongs to them.
Adapting existing software for a new client's data format Disqualified Known process applied to a new use case. No technical uncertainty.
Developing a new internal HR software tool Risky Internal-use software faces stricter rules. Must meet high-threshold-of-innovation test.
Running pilot production to meet a specific customer order Disqualified Production activity. Even if new, if the goal is filling an order it is not research.
Surveying customers to find out what features they want Disqualified Market research. Not technological in nature.
Developing a new manufacturing process that has never been done at your facility Qualifies New process, technical uncertainty about whether it will work, process of experimentation required.
Replicating a competitor's product from publicly available specs Disqualified Reverse engineering of funded research or known technology. Explicitly excluded.

What Good Documentation Actually Looks Like

The IRS is not looking for a perfect lab notebook. They are looking for evidence that the work was real, that it was technical, and that you tracked it. Here is what survives an audit.

Documentation that holds up

  • Project logs or tickets created at the time of the work, not reconstructed later
  • Design documents, technical specifications, and engineering drawings
  • Meeting notes or emails discussing technical problems and alternative approaches tested
  • Records of failed approaches, rejected prototypes, or abandoned design paths
  • Time tracking records tied to specific projects, even rough allocations recorded at the time
  • Payroll records that can be matched to employees who did qualifying work
  • Vendor invoices for supplies or contract research with descriptions of what was purchased
  • Test results, lab data, or trial records showing the process of experimentation
  • Git commit history or software version control logs for software development claims
  • Photos or video of prototype testing, equipment trials, or pilot runs
The single most protective thing you can do: Keep records as the work happens. A Slack thread from the day your engineer said "we tried three approaches and two failed" is worth more in an audit than a formal report written six months later.

The Funded Research Trap

This is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes businesses make with R&D credits. If someone else funded your research and bears the financial risk if it fails, you cannot claim the credit. The funder can claim it. You cannot.

This comes up constantly in government contracting, defense work, and client-funded development. If you signed a cost-plus contract where the client reimburses your R&D costs, that research is funded research. The client may have a credit claim. You do not.

It also comes up when a parent company funds a subsidiary's research. The subsidiary cannot claim the credit on research the parent funded and owns. Get clear on who bears the economic risk and who owns the results before you claim.

Contract language matters. If your development contract says the client owns all work product and you are reimbursed for costs, that is funded research. If you bear the risk and retain rights, it may not be. Have a specialist review your contracts before you claim research done under them.

The Internal-Use Software Problem

Software developed for your own internal operations faces a much higher bar than software developed for sale or license to others. The IRS requires internal-use software to meet a three-part test on top of the standard four-part test: it must be innovative (a significant reduction in cost or time), it must involve significant economic risk, and it must not be commercially available in a similar form.

CRM systems, accounting platforms, HR tools, and internal workflow software almost never meet this test. Off-the-shelf software that you customized definitely does not qualify. Software that you built for customers, or that creates a new technical capability that you then sell or license, has a much better shot.

What to Do If You Get Audited

An IRS examination of your R&D credit is not the end of the world. It is a documentation exercise. Here is the right response.

  1. Do not respond to the IRS without a specialist or CPA who has handled R&D audits before. General tax preparers are not the right people for this.
  2. Pull every piece of contemporaneous documentation you have: project logs, emails, time records, vendor invoices, test data.
  3. Be prepared to explain each research project in plain terms: what the technical uncertainty was, what alternatives were tested, and what the outcome was.
  4. If some activities in your claim are weak, do not fight for them. Concede the borderline items and defend the solid ones. Trying to hold every dollar invites deeper scrutiny.
  5. Know that IRS agents reviewing R&D credits vary widely in their technical knowledge. A specialist who has handled these exams knows how to communicate the technical substance of your claim in language that holds up.
If you are claiming the credit for the first time this year: Start your documentation now, before you file. Do not wait until you get a letter. The IRS allows refund claim examinations before they process the refund, and they are doing more of them.

The Bottom Line on Audit Risk

The R&D tax credit is legitimate, valuable, and worth claiming. The audit risk is real but manageable. Businesses that get into trouble are the ones that overclaim, under-document, and use preparers who do not specialize in this area.

A well-documented claim from a business doing genuine technical development work survives IRS scrutiny. The activities that get thrown out are almost always the ones that never should have been included in the first place. Claim what you actually did, document it like someone will ask, and work with people who do this for a living.
