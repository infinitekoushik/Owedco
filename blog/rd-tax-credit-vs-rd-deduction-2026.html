R&D Tax Credit vs R&D Deduction: Which Is Worth More in 2026?

By Owedco Research Team  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  10 min read

Most small business owners have heard of the R&D tax credit. Fewer know there's also an R&D deduction and that the rules around it changed sharply in 2022 and again in 2025. Even fewer know that you can often use both, or that the right combination depends entirely on your tax situation.

Here's the plain-English breakdown: what each one is, how they're calculated, what changed in 2022 and 2025 and how to figure out which strategy puts more money back in your pocket.

R&D Tax Credit

IRC Section 41

  • Dollar-for-dollar reduction in tax owed
  • Based on qualifying research expenses
  • Carries forward 20 years if unused
  • Can offset payroll tax (startups)
  • Reduces your tax bill directly
R&D Deduction

IRC Section 174

  • Reduces taxable income (not tax)
  • Broader set of qualifying expenses
  • Domestic costs deductible immediately again (Section 174A, tax years after 2024)
  • Foreign R&D still amortized over 15 years
  • Was amortized over 5 years for 2022 to 2024

The Core Difference: Credits vs. Deductions

A deduction reduces your taxable income. If you're in the 21% corporate tax bracket and you deduct $100,000 of R&D expenses, you save $21,000 in taxes.

A credit reduces your tax bill directly. A $21,000 R&D credit saves you $21,000 regardless of your tax bracket. Credits are almost always more valuable than deductions of the same dollar amount.

The Simple Rule

A $1 credit is worth more than a $1 deduction. A credit at the 21% tax rate is mathematically equivalent to a deduction of about $4.76. If you can get the credit, get the credit.

What Happened to Section 174 (And Where It Stands in 2026)

Before 2022, Section 174 let businesses immediately deduct 100% of R&D expenses in the year they occurred. From the 2022 tax year through 2024, domestic R&D expenses had to be amortized over 5 years (15 years for foreign R&D). Legislation signed in July 2025 created Section 174A, which lets you deduct domestic research costs immediately again for tax years beginning after 2024. Foreign research is still amortized over 15 years.

Pre-2022

Section 174 allowed 100% immediate expensing of R&D costs. Most small businesses had been doing this for decades.

2022: The Surprise Change

A provision from the 2017 tax law kicks in. Domestic R&D must now be amortized over 5 years. Many businesses owed unexpected taxes because they could only deduct 10% of their annual R&D spend in year 1.

2023 to mid 2025: Years of Failed Fixes

Multiple bills proposed to restore immediate expensing. For three tax years, businesses kept paying tax on money they had already spent.

July 2025 and 2026: Immediate Expensing Is Back

Section 174A lets you deduct domestic research costs in the year you spend them, for tax years beginning after 2024. Smaller businesses can generally go back and apply it to earlier years, and larger ones can recover unamortized balances faster. The rules for this are technical.

Have a CPA Handle the Transition

Whether you can apply the new rules to earlier years, and how to treat costs you already started amortizing, depends on your size and your filings. This is a place where a wrong election is expensive. Confirm the details with a CPA before you amend anything.

Can You Take Both the Credit and the Deduction?

Yes, but there's an adjustment. If you claim the Section 41 R&D tax credit, you must reduce your research deduction by the amount of the credit. You can avoid this by electing the "reduced credit" under IRC Section 280C, which lets you keep your full deduction in exchange for a smaller credit.

The 280C Election: When It Helps

The reduced credit is your credit multiplied by one minus the corporate rate (21%). The full credit costs you your own tax rate on the credit amount, through the lost deduction. So the full credit wins if your tax rate is below 21%, and the reduced credit wins if it is above 21%. Many S corporation and partnership owners are taxed above 21% on this income. Model both before you file.

Real Example: A $70,000 Credit, Two Ways to Take It

Take a simplified case: your credit comes to $70,000 before any adjustment. Compare what you keep after the deduction effect, at three different tax rates.

Full credit, deduction reduced by the credit

At a 15% tax rate ($70,000 x (1 - 15%)):$59,500
At a 21% tax rate ($70,000 x (1 - 21%)):$55,300
At a 37% tax rate ($70,000 x (1 - 37%)):$44,100

Reduced credit election (280C), full deduction kept

Reduced credit at any tax rate ($70,000 x (1 - 21%)):$55,300

What This Shows

At exactly 21% the two choices are identical. Below 21% the full credit keeps you about $4,200 more in this example. At 37% the reduced credit keeps about $11,200 more. Timing and whether you can use the credit this year matter too, so always model both.

Which Strategy Is Right for Your Business?

Use the full R&D credit (no 280C election) if:

  • You're pre-revenue or pre-profit and want to offset payroll taxes
  • Your tax rate is below 21%, so the deduction you give up is worth less than the credit
  • The credit is small compared with your total research spend

Use the reduced credit election (280C) if:

  • You're profitable and your tax rate on this income is above 21%
  • You will use the credit in the current year and want to keep the full deduction

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming your CPA has already claimed this. Most general-practice CPAs don't specialise in R&D credits. Ask explicitly.
  • Thinking you need a profit to benefit. Startups can use the payroll offset.
  • Claiming the credit without a proper study. An undocumented credit creates audit risk without a defensible position.
  • Missing retroactive claims. You can amend returns for the prior 3 years.
  • Not modeling the 280C election. A few hours of analysis can be worth tens of thousands of dollars.
