R&D Tax Credit for Aerospace Companies (2026 Guide)

Aerospace and defense companies do some of the most technically demanding research of any industry. They also face the most complicated R&D credit rules, mostly because of how government contracts work. Most aerospace businesses are leaving significant credits unclaimed because nobody explained the funded research rules correctly. This guide does that.

IR-funded vs self-funded
The single most important distinction in aerospace R&D credit claims. Get this wrong and you claim credits you do not own. Get it right and you may find an entirely separate pool of qualifying expenses nobody was tracking.

Why Aerospace Is Different From Every Other Industry

Most industries have one question to answer when claiming R&D credits: did this activity qualify under the four-part test? Aerospace has a second, equally important question: who funded this research?

The federal government funds a massive share of aerospace research through contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements. When the government funds research and bears the financial risk of failure, the business performing the research cannot claim the R&D credit on those activities. The government effectively already paid for the research through the contract price.

But most aerospace businesses also do work they fund themselves. Internal R&D, product improvements, new technology development using company money on company time. That work almost certainly qualifies. The problem is that many aerospace companies have never separated their government-funded and self-funded R&D for credit purposes. They either claim nothing because they assume all their R&D is government-funded, or they overclaim and include funded research that does not belong in the calculation.

The Funded Research Rule: How It Actually Works

Research is considered "funded" if you receive payment for the research regardless of whether the research succeeds. If your contract pays you your costs plus a fee whether or not the technology works, that is funded research and you cannot claim it.

Research is NOT funded if you bear the financial risk. If you spend your own money developing a technology without a contract guaranteeing payment, that is your research and your credit.

The contract type makes a significant difference here.

Cost-Plus-Fixed-Fee (CPFF)
Funded Research

Government reimburses all allowable costs plus a fixed fee. You bear no financial risk. This research does not qualify for the R&D credit.

Firm-Fixed-Price (FFP)
May Qualify

You bear cost overrun risk. If you spend more than the contract price, it comes out of your margin. The at-risk portion may qualify. Courts and IRS have split on this, get a specialist.

IR&D (Independent Research)
Qualifies

Company-funded research not tied to a specific contract. This is the cleanest qualifying category in aerospace. Fully eligible for the credit.

SBIR / STTR Grants
Partially

Phase I and II are typically funded research. Costs above the grant amount that you cover yourself may qualify. Phase III commercial work often qualifies more broadly.

OTA (Other Transaction Authority)
Depends

OTA agreements vary widely. Cost-sharing OTAs where you fund a portion of the research may create a qualifying pool on your share. Review the specific agreement.

Self-Funded Product Development
Qualifies

Technology you develop with your own capital, targeting commercial markets or speculative government programs, with no contract guaranteeing payment. Fully eligible.

What Qualifies: Aerospace-Specific Activities

Qualifies (self-funded)

  • New avionics system design and integration
  • Novel propulsion system development
  • Composite material structure design and testing
  • Thermal protection system development
  • Flight control algorithm development
  • Autonomous flight or guidance system R&D
  • Structural analysis software developed internally
  • Environmental control system engineering
  • EMI/EMC testing protocol development for new platforms
  • Novel fuel system or fuel efficiency technology
  • Manufacturing process development for new materials
  • Satellite payload technology development
  • Non-destructive testing method innovation
  • Ground support equipment designed for new aircraft

Does Not Qualify

  • Research fully funded under CPFF or cost-reimbursement contracts
  • Routine maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO) activities
  • Production of flight hardware under existing qualified designs
  • Compliance testing required by existing FAA or MIL-SPEC standards
  • Quality assurance of existing manufacturing processes
  • Reverse engineering foreign military hardware
  • Research performed outside the US or its territories
  • Administrative or program management activities on contracts

Real Scenarios: Qualifying vs. Disqualified

Activity Status Reason
Engineers spend 30% of time on an IR&D program developing a lighter landing gear design Qualifies Self-funded, technical uncertainty, systematic design iterations. Classic qualifying activity.
Full engineering team on a CPFF contract developing a new radar system for the Air Force Funded Government bears the risk, reimburses costs. You cannot claim this research.
Firm-fixed-price contract to develop a new drone airframe where you exceeded budget by $2M Partial The at-risk overrun may qualify. The base contract amount is more complex. Needs specialist review.
Software team develops a novel flight simulation tool for internal use across multiple programs Qualifies Not internal-use software in the narrow IRS sense. Directly supports qualified research activities.
Manufacturing engineers develop a new friction stir welding process for titanium components Qualifies New manufacturing process, technical uncertainty about weld integrity and parameters, process of experimentation.
Routine FAA certification testing on a design already finalized Disqualified Compliance testing of a completed design does not eliminate technical uncertainty about the design itself.
SBIR Phase II research where company contributes 20% cost share Partial The 20% company-funded portion may qualify. The government-funded 80% does not.

The IR&D Budget: Your Cleanest Credit Opportunity

Independent Research and Development spending is the most straightforward category for aerospace companies. IR&D is research you fund yourself, not tied to a specific contract, aimed at developing future capabilities or products.

Most aerospace companies already track IR&D separately for government accounting purposes under DCAA and FAR Part 31. If you have an IR&D budget that you report to the government, virtually every dollar of qualified technical work in that budget is a candidate for the R&D tax credit.

The catch is that companies often think of IR&D as something only large prime contractors do. Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers, MRO businesses with engineering departments, avionics shops, and small UAV developers all do self-funded technical research that qualifies. If you are spending engineering hours on work that is not tied to a paying contract, that is IR&D and it needs to be in your credit calculation.

If you file incurred cost submissions with DCAA: Your IR&D pool is already separated from direct contract costs. That same separation is the starting point for your R&D credit calculation. You have already done most of the work.

The Space and Commercial Aviation Opportunity

Commercial space has changed the landscape for aerospace R&D credits. Companies developing launch vehicles, satellite buses, reentry systems, or in-space propulsion for commercial customers are doing almost entirely self-funded research with no government cost-reimbursement contract standing between them and the credit.

The same applies to commercial aviation companies developing new aircraft systems, sustainable aviation fuel compatibility work, electric propulsion for urban air mobility, and advanced avionics for business aviation. These are not government programs. They are commercial product development. The funded research exclusion does not apply and the full suite of qualifying activities is available.

Urban air mobility, advanced air mobility, and eVTOL development in particular represent some of the highest-QRE-to-revenue ratios of any sector right now. These companies are spending enormous percentages of their budget on engineering work with uncertain outcomes. The R&D credit was built for exactly this situation.

Wages: The Biggest Category and the Hardest to Track

In aerospace, engineering wages dominate qualifying research expenses. The challenge is that aerospace engineers move between government-funded programs and self-funded work, sometimes within the same week. You need time allocation records that reflect this accurately.

DCAA-compliant timekeeping systems already separate direct contract labor from IR&D and overhead. If you have this system in place, your time allocation records for the R&D credit are already substantially built. If you do not, and you are doing mixed government and commercial work, you need to implement project-level time tracking before you can support a credit claim.

One thing aerospace companies often miss: the engineers doing proposal work on new speculative programs may have qualifying time. If the proposal involves developing a technical approach to an unsolved problem and that technical work is genuinely uncertain, portions of that activity can qualify. Proposal management and pricing do not. The technical development underlying a bid can.

The security clearance question: Classified program work can still qualify for the R&D credit. The credit calculation does not require you to disclose classified information to claim it. A specialist with aerospace experience knows how to handle this. Do not skip the credit because your work is classified.

Documentation for Aerospace Claims

Aerospace companies generally have better documentation than almost any other industry because government contracting demands it. The challenge is pointing that documentation at the right activities for credit purposes.

What you need for a defensible aerospace R&D credit claim:

  • Contract type documentation for every program (CPFF, FFP, OTA, IR&D designation)
  • Timekeeping records showing which employees worked on which programs and how many hours
  • IR&D project descriptions identifying the technical uncertainty being addressed
  • Engineering notebooks, design review records, or test reports showing process of experimentation
  • Records of technical alternatives evaluated and rejected
  • Cost accounting records separating direct contract costs from IR&D and overhead
  • For FFP contracts being partially claimed: documentation of the at-risk financial exposure

How Much Can an Aerospace Company Claim

It depends entirely on how much self-funded research you do. A company with a substantial IR&D program and $3 million in annual qualifying wages and supplies could have an annual federal credit between $200,000 and $400,000 depending on their base period calculation.

For commercial space and commercial aviation companies where nearly all R&D is self-funded, the numbers scale directly with your engineering headcount. A 50-person engineering team at an average fully-loaded cost of $180,000 per person, with 60% of their time on qualifying activities, has $5.4 million in QREs. At 14% ASC that is a $756,000 federal credit. Per year. Every year.

Most aerospace companies that have never claimed are sitting on three years of amendable returns. Do the math on what you have left unclaimed before you decide whether this is worth your attention.
