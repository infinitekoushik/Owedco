R&D Tax Credit vs R&D Deduction: Which Is Worth More in 2026?

By Owedco Research Team  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  10 min read

Most small business owners have heard of the R&D tax credit. Fewer know there's also an R&D deduction and that Congress has been fighting over it for years. Even fewer know that you can often use both or that the right combination depends entirely on your tax situation.

Here's the plain-English breakdown: what each one is, how they're calculated, what changed in 2022 (and what may change again in 2026) and how to figure out which strategy puts more money back in your pocket.

R&D Tax Credit

IRC Section 41

  • Dollar-for-dollar reduction in tax owed
  • Based on qualifying research expenses
  • Carries forward 20 years if unused
  • Can offset payroll tax (startups)
  • Reduces your tax bill directly
R&D Deduction

IRC Section 174

  • Reduces taxable income (not tax)
  • Broader set of qualifying expenses
  • Now requires 5-year amortization (US)
  • 15-year amortization for foreign R&D
  • Contested in Congress since 2022

The Core Difference: Credits vs. Deductions

This is the most important thing to understand before anything else:

A deduction reduces your taxable income. If you're in the 21% corporate tax bracket and you deduct $100,000 of R&D expenses, you save $21,000 in taxes. The value of the deduction depends on your tax rate.

A credit reduces your tax bill directly. A $21,000 R&D credit saves you $21,000 in taxes regardless of your tax bracket. Credits are almost always more valuable than deductions of the same dollar amount.

The Simple Rule

A $1 credit is worth more than a $1 deduction. A credit at the 21% tax rate is mathematically equivalent to a deduction of about $4.76. If you can get the credit, get the credit.

What the Section 174 Change Did (And Why It Still Matters in 2026)

Before 2022, Section 174 let businesses immediately deduct 100% of their R&D expenses in the year they occurred. This was extremely valuable — full expensing, dollar one.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 included a provision — delayed until 2022 — that eliminated immediate expensing. Starting with the 2022 tax year, domestic R&D expenses under Section 174 must be amortized over 5 years (15 years for foreign R&D). This was a massive and largely unannounced change that caught thousands of businesses off guard when they filed their 2022 returns.

Pre-2022

Section 174 allowed 100% immediate expensing of R&D costs. Most small businesses had been doing this for decades.

2022 — The Surprise Change

TCJA provision kicks in. Domestic R&D must now be amortized over 5 years. Many businesses owed unexpected taxes because they could only deduct 10% of their annual R&D spend (half-year convention in year 1).

2023–2025 — Congressional Gridlock

Multiple bills proposed to restore immediate expensing. None passed into law. Businesses continued amortizing.

2026 — Current Status

Section 174 amortization remains in effect. Legislative proposals are ongoing. Consult a tax advisor for the latest status — this is actively contested territory.

⚠️ Check With Your Tax Advisor on Current 174 Status

The Section 174 rules have been subject to ongoing legislative proposals. The status as of your filing date may differ from what's described here. This is one area where a qualified CPA is not optional.

Can You Take Both the Credit and the Deduction?

Yes but there's an adjustment. If you claim the Section 41 R&D tax credit, you must reduce your Section 174 deduction by the amount of the credit. This prevents a double benefit on the same dollars.

You can avoid this reduction by electing the "reduced credit" under IRC Section 280C. This lets you keep your full deduction but uses a reduced credit rate (roughly 13% instead of 20% for the regular method or about 12.1% instead of 14% for the ASC). For many businesses, the reduced credit election — keeping the full deduction is the better math.

The 280C Election: When It Helps

If you're a profitable business in a high tax bracket, the reduced credit election (keeping your full deduction) often produces a better combined outcome than taking the full credit and reducing your deduction. Your tax advisor should model both scenarios before you file.

Real Example: $500k in R&D Spending, Two Scenarios

Let's say you're a profitable software company with $500,000 in qualifying R&D expenses and a 21% effective tax rate. Here's what each approach looks like:

Scenario A — Credit Only (Full Rate, No Deduction Reduction)

Section 41 ASC Credit (14% × $500k): +$70,000
Section 174 Deduction Reduction ($70k × 21%): −$14,700
Net Tax Benefit: $55,300

Scenario B — Reduced Credit Election (280C)

Reduced ASC Credit (~12.1% × $500k): +$60,500
Full Section 174 Deduction retained (21%): +$21,000*
Net Tax Benefit: $81,500

*Deduction value shown as the incremental benefit retained by not reducing the §174 deduction. Actual benefit depends on your full tax situation. This is illustrative only — not tax advice.

Verdict on This Example

The reduced credit election (Scenario B) produces roughly $26,000 more in net tax benefit for a profitable company at 21%. But this flips for companies with low taxable income or those using the payroll tax offset. Always model both.

Which Strategy Is Right for Your Business?

Use the full R&D credit (no 280C election) if:

  • You're pre-revenue or pre-profit and want to offset payroll taxes
  • You have significant credit carryforwards and want to maximize the dollar amount
  • Your effective tax rate is low (under ~18%), making the deduction less valuable
  • You have minimal Section 174 expenses and the deduction reduction is small

Use the reduced credit election (280C) if:

  • You're a profitable business with a meaningful tax rate (21%+)
  • You have large R&D expenses and the deduction is worth keeping
  • You want simplicity — one election covers the math

Neither is worth much if:

  • Your research activities don't meet the four-part test
  • You don't have contemporaneous documentation
  • Your R&D is primarily funded by grants or government contracts (excluded from §41)

What About State R&D Credits?

Most states with income taxes offer their own R&D credits, typically calculated independently from the federal credit. California (24% for small businesses), New York (9%), Massachusetts (10%), and Texas (no income tax, but strong talent incentives) are the most significant.

State credits generally can't offset payroll taxes — they reduce state income tax only. But stacked on top of the federal credit, state R&D programs can meaningfully increase the total benefit.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming your CPA has already claimed this. Most general-practice CPAs don't specialize in R&D credits. Ask explicitly whether you've been claiming it.
  • Thinking you need a profit to benefit. Startups can use the payroll offset. Pre-revenue companies can still generate credits that carry forward.
  • Claiming the credit without a proper study. An undocumented credit is worse than no credit — it creates audit risk without a defensible position.
  • Missing retroactive claims. You can amend tax returns for the prior 3 years. If you've been doing qualifying research and not claiming, that money is still recoverable.
  • Not modeling the 280C election. Many businesses file without comparing the two scenarios. A few hours of analysis can be worth tens of thousands of dollars.

Bottom Line

The R&D tax credit is almost always more valuable than the R&D deduction on a per-dollar basis — but the interaction between the two (the §280C election) means the right strategy depends on your specific tax situation.

If you're a profitable company with meaningful R&D spend, model both scenarios before filing. If you're an early-stage company without profits, focus on the credit and the payroll tax offset.

Either way: if you've been doing qualifying research and haven't been claiming anything, that's money you're leaving on the table — retroactively recoverable for up to three years.
