R&D Tax Credit for Architecture and Construction Firms (2026 Guide)

You solve technical problems on every project. Structural conflicts, site constraints, systems that do not fit together on paper. The R&D credit exists for exactly that kind of work, and most architecture, engineering and construction firms never claim it because someone told them it is only for labs. That someone was wrong, and it has been costing you real money.

Fixed price beats cost plus
In AEC, your contract decides whether the credit survives an audit. If the client pays you no matter what and owns everything you develop, that work is weak. If you carry the risk, it is strong.

Why AEC firms skip the credit

The credit has a reputation problem. Software companies and manufacturers claim it every year. Architects, engineers and contractors assume their work is too routine, or that the paperwork is not worth it, or that claiming it invites an audit.

Part of that fear is earned. The IRS does challenge claims from design and construction firms. But those challenges go after thin documentation, not the idea that your industry can qualify. Firms that keep records get paid. Firms that guess do not.

What the credit is worth

The federal credit is a dollar for dollar reduction of your tax bill. It is not a deduction. The regular method is 20 percent of qualified research expenses above a base amount. The alternative simplified method is 14 percent of qualified expenses above half of your prior three year average. Most small firms use the simplified method because it is far easier to compute.

Qualified expenses include wages for the people doing the work, supplies used in it, and a portion of what you pay outside contractors to do it. If your firm owes little or no income tax, a qualified small business can apply part of the credit against payroll taxes instead. That turns it into cash instead of a carryforward you wait years to use.

You can go back. If you never claimed it, you can generally amend the last three open tax years and claim the credit for each year where you had qualifying work. Every year you wait is a year you may lose for good.

The four part test, applied to your work

Every claim lives or dies on four questions. Answer them project by project, not for the company as a whole.

  1. Permitted purpose. You were trying to improve the function, performance, reliability or quality of a design, process or system.
  2. Technological in nature. The solution relied on engineering, physics, materials science or computer science.
  3. Technical uncertainty. At the start you did not know the capability, the method, or the right design.
  4. Process of experimentation. You evaluated alternatives through modeling, simulation, testing or trial and revision.

Often qualifies

  • Custom structural or facade systems with no standard solution
  • Energy, thermal and daylight modeling to hit a performance target
  • Testing alternative means and methods when the standard approach would not work
  • Custom BIM scripts, parametric tools and in house software
  • Prototype assemblies and mockups that get tested and revised
  • Value engineering that required real technical analysis of alternatives
  • Novel foundation, shoring or erection methods for difficult sites

Usually does not

  • Routine drafting and code compliance checks
  • Aesthetic choices with no technical uncertainty
  • Standard details you have used a hundred times
  • Work after the design is finished and in production
  • Project management and administration
  • Anything the client funds with no risk to you and owns outright

Your contracts can kill the claim

This is where AEC firms get burned. Research funded by a client is excluded from the credit. Two things decide it. First, whether you get paid regardless of whether the work succeeds. Second, who owns the rights to what you develop. If the contract says the client pays no matter what and keeps all the IP, your claim for that project is weak.

Read your contract templates before you read anything else. Fixed price work where you eat the overruns is much stronger than cost plus work where the client does. If you can, negotiate to keep rights to your methods and tools. That is also good business, separate from taxes.

Do not do this

Do not estimate your research percentage at year end and call it done. Flat percentages with no project records are the first thing an examiner picks apart. Track time against specific projects while the work happens.

What to document, starting this week

The IRS does not accept memory. It accepts records created while the work happened.

  • A project list that names the technical problem on each job in one sentence
  • Time records tying each person to specific projects
  • Design iterations, calculations, test results, simulation runs and rejected alternatives. Your failures are your evidence.
  • Contracts showing who carried the risk and who owns the IP
  • Invoices for outside contractors and supplies tied to the same projects

The Section 174 mess

For several years, domestic research costs had to be amortized over five years, which made the credit painful to use. Legislation passed in 2025 restored immediate expensing of domestic research costs for tax years beginning after 2024. How that interacts with your credit and any amended returns depends on your situation, so confirm the details with your CPA before filing.

How you actually claim it

The credit is claimed on IRS Form 6765, attached to your return. Your CPA files it. Your job is to hand them project level evidence they can defend. A clean file is the difference between a credit and a letter from the IRS.
